## 1. Technical Approach
*Budget: 3.09 pages / ~1,697 words — weight 29, the second-heaviest criterion*

### 1.1 Identifying and evaluating critical findings

A critical finding is not a severity score. It is a judgement that a structural
or safety condition poses an immediate or potential danger to the travelling
public, and that judgement triggers an obligation to notify the State and to act
before the inspection report is ever written. Ocotillo trains every team leader
to treat the two as separate acts: condition rating is documentation, and a
critical finding is a decision. Confusing them is the most common failure we see
in inspection programmes, because it delays notification while an inspector
completes paperwork.

Our criteria are applied in a fixed order. First, is there section loss,
cracking, settlement, displacement or connection distress in a **fracture-
critical or non-redundant member**, or in a member whose failure would remove
support from the deck? Second, is the observed condition **changing** — is there
evidence of recent movement, fresh fracture surface, active spalling, or a
change from the previous cycle beyond what deterioration alone explains? Third,
does the condition affect **load path or public access** — a leaning column, a
compromised bent cap, a settled abutment, a damaged rail on a high-speed
facility, or debris and scour undermining a foundation. A yes to the first
question combined with a yes to either of the others is a critical finding.

Distinguishing routine deficiencies from true critical findings is the harder
half, and we are explicit with our inspectors about the asymmetry. A team leader
who escalates a condition that turns out to be routine has cost the State a
review. A team leader who defers a condition that turns out to be critical has
cost something that cannot be recovered. We therefore instruct escalation on
uncertainty, and we absorb the review cost internally rather than passing it on
as a change. What prevents that instruction from producing constant false alarms
is not reluctance but evidence: the inspector must photograph the condition,
measure it, compare it against the previous cycle's record on the spot, and
speak to the team leader before leaving the site. Most routine deficiencies
resolve themselves at the comparison step, because the record shows the
condition has been stable for three cycles.

**One example.** During a routine cycle on a four-span prestressed concrete
girder bridge over a farm-to-market crossing, our inspector recorded a
diagonal crack in the web of an exterior girder near the bearing. The crack
itself was within the range our inspectors see regularly and, taken alone, would
have been a condition-state entry. Two things changed the assessment. The
previous cycle's photographs showed no crack at that location, and efflorescence
staining at the crack was fresh and wet against a dry surrounding surface,
indicating active water movement rather than an old, sealed feature. The
inspector measured the crack at 0.035 inches and found the bearing pad rolled
approximately three-quarters of an inch out of position, which explained the web
stress as a restraint problem rather than a flexural one. The team leader was on
site within the hour, classified the condition as a critical finding on the
basis of active change in a member with limited redundancy, and notified the
district the same afternoon. Interim load posting went up within two days and a
bearing replacement followed. The point of the example is not the diagnosis; it
is that the crack alone was routine, and the *comparison against the previous
cycle* is what made it critical. That comparison is only possible when
inspectors carry prior records into the field, which is a programme design
decision, not an inspector's initiative.

### 1.2 Bridge load ratings across structure types

Our load rating work is performed under the AASHTO Manual for Bridge Evaluation
using load and resistance factor rating, with allowable stress or load factor
methods retained where a structure's original design basis and the State's
direction make them the appropriate comparison. Ratings are produced by
engineers who also read inspection reports, which is the arrangement we consider
non-negotiable: a rating engineer working only from plans will rate a structure
that no longer exists in the condition drawn.

For **simple-span** structures — the majority of the statewide inventory — the
analysis is well bounded, and our attention goes to the inputs rather than the
model: as-built versus as-designed strand counts, measured section loss,
deterioration of bearing seats, and the actual condition of the deck acting
compositely or not. For **continuous-span** structures, negative moment regions
over interior supports govern more often than midspan, and deck cracking and
reinforcement corrosion in those regions change the rating materially; we also
check whether the assumed continuity is still real, because a deteriorated
diaphragm or a failed link slab can leave a structure behaving as a series of
simple spans under load. For **non-typical** configurations — curved or skewed
girders, trusses, through-girder and steel bent structures, spans on flexible or
timber substructure, and any structure with a fracture-critical member — we
model explicitly rather than by analogy, and we do not carry forward a rating
from a nominally similar bridge.

**One example.** On a three-span continuous steel plate girder structure, our
inspection recorded measured section loss at the bottom flange of the fascia
girder near an interior support, in a location where deck drainage had been
discharging directly onto the steel for an extended period. The initial reflex
in many programmes is to apply an average loss factor across the flange. Our
rating engineer instead used the inspector's measured thickness readings, taken
at a grid rather than at a single worst point, and modelled the reduced section
over its actual measured length. The result mattered in both directions: the
loss was more severe than an averaged assumption at the worst location, and less
extensive along the girder than a uniform reduction would have implied. Rating
on measured geometry produced an inventory rating that permitted the structure
to remain in service unposted, where the averaged assumption would have produced
a posting that the structure did not warrant. Accuracy was confirmed by an
independent check performed by a second rating engineer who had not seen the
first model, and by returning to the field to re-measure the four readings that
most influenced the result — a step we build into the schedule whenever the
rating outcome sits close to a posting threshold.

### 1.3 Bridge scour evaluation and assessment

Scour is the condition our programme treats with the most structured process,
because it is the failure mode least visible during a routine above-water
inspection and the one most likely to change between cycles. Identification
begins with the record: scour-critical coding, any existing plan of action, and
the structure's history of channel movement. In the field, inspectors probe and
sound at every substructure unit in water, record streambed elevations against
the established reference, and photograph the channel upstream and downstream
for evidence of migration, degradation, contraction at the opening, and debris
accumulation. Where the record and the field disagree, the field governs and the
discrepancy is reported.

Analysis considers the factors that decide whether observed scour threatens the
structure rather than merely the appearance of the channel: foundation type and
depth relative to the measured streambed, whether the foundation is on spread
footings or piling, the elevation of any footing relative to the estimated scour
depth, the erodibility of the material actually present, and the hydraulic
behaviour of the opening including pressure flow and overtopping in larger
events. A pile-supported bent in erodible soil with a footing already exposed is
a different problem from a shallow footing in a stable channel, even when the
measured scour hole is the same depth.

Monitoring is where scour programmes usually fail, and we design for it
explicitly. Bridges with an active plan of action get a defined inspection
trigger — a flood stage, a rainfall total, or an elapsed interval — and the
responsibility for watching that trigger sits with a named person in our office
rather than with the field crew who may be four hundred miles away. Post-flood
inspection is scheduled from gauge data, not from a request. Where a structure's
condition warrants it, we recommend fixed reference monitoring so that
successive readings are comparable rather than approximate, and we report
changes against the reference rather than as fresh observations.

### 1.4 Delivering this statewide

The technical approach above is uniform; the delivery is regional. Ocotillo
performs inspection directly in Central Texas and manages three regional
subproviders covering West, East and North Texas, each staffed by inspectors who
live in the region they inspect. Statewide coverage assembled from a single
office produces long windshield time, fatigue and cycle slippage. Coverage
assembled from resident regional teams produces consistency problems instead —
which is a solvable problem, and Section 3 describes the quality control system
that solves it.

One further element of the approach applies across all three of the areas above.
Every inspection begins with the previous cycle's report and photographs in the
inspector's hands, in the field, at the structure. This sounds administrative and
is in fact the single highest-value technical practice in the programme. The
critical finding in Section 1.1 was identified by comparison, not by observation.
The load rating in Section 1.2 was correct because measured readings were taken
against a known prior geometry. Scour is meaningless as a single reading and
meaningful only as a change against a reference. In each case the judgement
depends on the record being present at the moment of decision rather than
retrieved afterwards, and we build the mobile record access that makes that
possible into the programme rather than leaving it to each crew.

### 2.2 Approach to managing this programme

Her approach rests on the view that a routine inspection programme is a
scheduling problem wrapped around an engineering judgement problem, and that the
two fail differently. Engineering judgement fails quietly, one inspector at a
time, and is caught only by review. Scheduling fails visibly and late, when a
due date passes. She manages the first through the quality control system in
Section 3 and the second through a rolling twelve-month plan that is never
allowed to become a year-end sprint.

**The anticipated inspection schedule.** Field inspection is planned Monday
through Thursday, typically 7:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m., with Friday reserved for
report completion, quality review and rescheduling. Ten-hour field days across
four days reduce mobilisation cost per structure in a statewide inventory where
travel dominates, and the reserved Friday is what keeps report backlog from
accumulating — a backlog that, once it forms, is paid for at the end of the
cycle when there is no time left. Crews work seasonally weighted schedules,
front-loading the northern and western regions in spring and autumn and
accepting summer heat constraints in the south, which is a safety decision as
much as a productivity one.

Night and lane-closure work is scheduled where traffic control requires it,
principally on high-volume urban structures where daytime closure is not
permitted. Marisol schedules these as discrete campaigns rather than
interspersing them, because rotating crews between day and night work within a
week degrades both attention and safety.

**Availability for coordination.** Marisol is reachable during standard business
hours and is the single point of contact for the State on scheduling, findings
and reporting. Critical findings are escalated immediately regardless of hour.
She holds a standing weekly coordination slot with the State's programme
contact, whether or not there is business, on the reasoning that a standing slot
that is sometimes short is more useful than a meeting that gets scheduled only
when something has gone wrong. Quality assurance activity by the State is
supported by giving advance notice of crew locations for the coming fortnight,
so that a State reviewer can join an inspection in the field without
arrangement.

### 2.3 Inspection access methods

Access determines both the safety and the completeness of an inspection, and a
programme that lacks an access method will produce inspections that quietly omit
what could not be reached. Ocotillo maintains and its subproviders supply: ladder
and rigging access for low structures; an under-bridge inspection unit for
structures over water, deep crossings and high-volume facilities where hands-on
access to the superstructure is otherwise impossible; boat access for water
crossings where the channel permits; rope access for confined and high steel
where an under-bridge unit cannot reach; and unmanned aircraft for reconnaissance
and for documenting conditions at height in advance, so that hands-on access is
planned to the right locations rather than discovered on site.

Unmanned aircraft supplement hands-on inspection and never substitute for it
where hands-on access is required. We are explicit about this because the
efficiency case for drones is real and the temptation to over-claim is
correspondingly strong. Their genuine value in this programme is planning:
knowing before mobilising a snooper exactly which spans need it.

Access is also a scheduling constraint, not only an equipment question, and
Marisol manages it as one. Under-bridge inspection units are a shared and
limited resource across four firms and a statewide inventory; structures
requiring one are batched geographically and scheduled as campaigns, with
traffic control arranged for the campaign rather than per structure. Structures
requiring boat access are scheduled against seasonal water levels. Planning
access at the programme level rather than the crew level is what keeps a
specialist resource from becoming the reason a due date slips.

## 3. Project Planning and Management
*Budget: 2.13 pages / ~1,170 words — weight 20*

### 3.1 Staffing: who, how and why

The team is built around resident regional capability under central engineering
control. Ocotillo, as prime, performs the project management category in full
and a quarter of the inspection work, self-performing 45% of the contract
against a 30% minimum. Three subproviders each hold a defined region: Pecos
Valley Bridge Inspection in West Texas, Neches River Structural Services in East
Texas, and Panhandle Structures Group across the Panhandle and North.

The reason for this shape is travel. In a statewide inventory, the dominant cost
and the dominant cause of schedule slippage is the distance between a crew and
the next structure. Regional resident crews cut that distance; they also bring
familiarity with local channel behaviour, local traffic patterns and local
district staff. The cost of the arrangement is variation between regions, and
that cost is paid down through the quality control system described below rather
than absorbed.

Each region's work is led by a Professional Engineer resident in that region.
Team leaders meet the National Bridge Inspection Standards qualification
requirements, and their qualifications, training currency and refresher
intervals are tracked centrally by our office rather than by each subprovider,
so that a lapse is visible to us before it is visible to the State.

### 3.2 Communication plan

Communication runs on a fixed rhythm so that its absence is noticeable. Weekly:
a coordination slot with the State's programme contact and an internal crew
call covering the coming fortnight's schedule, access requirements and traffic
control needs. Monthly: a programme status report showing structures completed
against plan, reports delivered against due date, findings by severity, and any
structure at risk of missing its due date, with the corrective action already
taken rather than proposed. Immediately: critical findings, safety incidents,
and any event that will move a due date.

The monthly report deliberately shows structures at risk rather than structures
already late. A report of what has already gone wrong is a record; a report of
what is about to go wrong is a decision point. Our project manager holds the
list of at-risk structures personally.

### 3.3 Quality control

Quality control operates at three levels and each catches a different failure.

**In the field**, the team leader reviews every inspection before the crew
leaves the site, with the previous cycle's report and photographs in hand. This
catches the omission and the changed-condition failure — the two errors that
cannot be caught later, because by then the crew is four hundred miles away.

**In the office**, a qualified reviewer who did not perform the inspection
checks every report for completeness, internal consistency, consistency with the
previous cycle, and correct condition-state and appraisal coding. Reports where
the coding has changed from the previous cycle receive additional scrutiny,
because an unexplained change is either a real deterioration that needs
narrative support or a coding inconsistency between inspectors.

**Across the programme**, our project manager samples completed reports each
month across all four firms and compares coding of similar conditions between
regions. This is the check that exists specifically because of the regional
delivery model. Two competent inspectors in different regions will drift apart
in their coding of the same condition over a year, and no report-level review
will detect it, because each report is internally correct. Comparing across
regions is the only way to see it. Where drift is found, it is corrected by
joint field calibration — the inspectors involved inspect the same structure
together — rather than by memorandum.

### 3.4 Use of subproviders and meeting commitments

Ocotillo's practice with subproviders is to give each a defined region with
defined structures rather than dispatching them to overflow work. A subprovider
with a region owns a schedule, a due-date record and a relationship with a
district; a subprovider used as surge capacity owns none of those and behaves
accordingly. Percentages of work are committed at proposal and honoured; where
a region's actual volume runs below its commitment, work is transferred with the
subprovider's agreement rather than withdrawn.

Each subprovider's team leader qualifications, insurance and precertification
status are verified by us before work is authorised and re-verified annually.
Invoices are reviewed against work actually completed, and subproviders are paid
on the schedule the contract requires, which we state plainly because prompt
payment is the single strongest factor in retaining a good subprovider across a
multi-year contract.

### 3.5 Mobilisation and records

Mobilisation on an indefinite deliverable contract is continuous rather than
single, because each work authorisation begins its own small mobilisation. We
therefore stand the programme up once and keep it standing: regional crews,
equipment and traffic control arrangements are in place before the first
authorisation, and a new authorisation draws on them rather than assembling
them. In practice this means the first inspection under a new authorisation
happens in days rather than weeks.

Records are held centrally from the first day. Inspection history, photographs,
access notes, traffic control plans, district contacts and equipment
requirements for every structure live in one system that all four firms use, not
in four systems that are reconciled. The reason is the one given in Section 3.3:
a regional delivery model produces divergence unless something holds the centre,
and a shared record is the cheapest thing that does. It is also what makes the
cross-region coding comparison possible at all, since comparing regions requires
their reports to sit side by side.

### 3.6 Resource management and continuity

The programme's binding resource is qualified team leaders, not inspectors. We
maintain a deliberate surplus: more qualified team leaders than the minimum
required to staff the cycle, so that the loss of one is absorbed rather than
escalated. Team leader qualification is a pipeline we manage continuously,
moving experienced inspectors through the required training and documented
experience rather than recruiting under pressure when a departure occurs.

Turnover is planned for rather than hoped against. Each region has a named
second who is qualified to lead it. Records — inspection history, photographs,
access notes, district contacts — are held centrally, not on a crew's laptop, so
that a departure costs a person and not a body of knowledge. A replacement lead
inherits a region's full history on their first day, which is the difference
between a handover and a restart, and it is why we have never had to extend a
due date because of a personnel change.
