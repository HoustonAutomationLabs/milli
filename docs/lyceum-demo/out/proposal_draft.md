## 1. Technical Approach
*Budget: 2.84 pages / ~1,560 words*

### 1.1 Understanding the project

This contract covers construction engineering and inspection for the four remaining direct connectors at the SH 45 and IH 35 interchange, CSJ 0015-10-073, in the Austin District: northbound IH 35 to westbound SH 45, northbound IH 35 to eastbound SH 45, westbound SH 45 to southbound IH 35, and eastbound SH 45 to southbound IH 35. The construction scope combines retaining walls, elevated bridge structures, road widening and pavement overlay.

Three characteristics of this work drive our approach, and each one changes how the inspection team is built.

**The work is elevated, and it is over live traffic.** Direct connectors are constructed above one of the busiest corridors in Texas while that corridor stays open. Drilled shafts, columns, bent caps, girder sets and deck placements happen over or immediately adjacent to traffic that does not stop. Inspection cannot be scheduled around the contractor's convenience; it has to be present when the work occurs, which for girder erection and deck placement means nights and weekends. Our staffing plan is built around that reality rather than adapted to it after award.

**The four connectors are four sequences, not one.** Each connector has its own foundation, substructure, superstructure and approach work, and the contractor will run them concurrently at different stages. At any given week the project may have drilled shafts on one connector, bent cap forming on a second, girders on a third and approach embankment on a fourth. An inspection team organised as a single undifferentiated pool loses continuity; an inspector who watched a shaft go in is the right person to watch the column that sits on it.

**The interchange is surrounded by concurrent work.** The RFP asks specifically about coordinating with adjacent, concurrent projects, and this location warrants the question. Work in this corridor is rarely isolated: adjacent contracts, utility relocations and maintenance operations share haul routes, lane closure windows, and in some cases the same TxDOT area office staff. Conflicts here are scheduling conflicts before they are engineering conflicts.

### 1.2 Approach to the work

**Engineer Led Inspection Teams organised by connector.** We propose four ELITs, one per direct connector, each with a lead inspector who stays with that structure from foundation through deck and approach. A fifth roadway team covers widening, overlay and the shared mainlane work. Materials, survey, geotechnical and utility specialists are shared across all five and dispatched by activity rather than assigned to a structure. This gives every structure a continuous set of eyes while keeping specialist utilisation efficient.

**Deficiency identification within 24 hours, by design not by effort.** Attachment C requires that deficiencies in the contractor's work be identified and reported to the State within 24 hours of obtaining knowledge. We treat that as a workflow requirement rather than a diligence requirement. Every ELIT lead closes each shift with a deficiency log entry — including a positive "none observed" — which the Project Manager reviews the following morning. Anything logged as a deficiency generates a written notice to the Area Engineer the same day it is reviewed. The commitment is met because the process cannot complete without meeting it.

**Testing that stays ahead of placement.** Materials testing on a connector project fails in one direction: results arrive after the concrete has set. We schedule plant inspection and placement inspection from the contractor's three-week look-ahead rather than from the daily schedule, and our materials task leader confirms plant readiness and technician availability before each placement is authorised. Where proficiency samples or calibration checks are approaching expiry, they are completed in advance of the placement window, not during it.

**Documentation as it happens.** Records built at the end of a project are reconstructions. Our record keeper works from daily inputs, and the closeout package is assembled continuously so that final documentation is a review exercise rather than an archaeology exercise.

### 1.3 Managing the likely challenges

**Utility conflicts.** Interchange reconstruction reliably surfaces utilities that are not where the plans put them. We hold subsurface utility engineering and utility construction management on the team rather than sourcing them after a conflict appears, so the interval between discovery and a proposed work-around is days rather than weeks. Attachment C asks the Engineer to provide work-around ideas that keep the project moving during utility or right-of-way conflict; having the utility lead already mobilised is what makes that possible.

**Differing site conditions.** Drilled shafts in this corridor encounter variable material. Our geotechnical task leader is available for same-week evaluation, and our claims and scheduling leads assess time and cost impact in parallel with the technical evaluation, so the State receives one coordinated recommendation.

**Schedule impact and claims exposure.** Elevated work over live traffic concentrates risk in a small number of critical activities. We maintain an independent understanding of the contractor's critical path and evaluate every proposed change against it, so that when a change order is negotiated the State already knows what it is worth in time as well as dollars.

**Night and weekend work.** Girder erection and deck placement will occur under closure windows at night. We staff night operations with the same inspectors who cover the structure during the day where shift rules allow, and where they do not, with a designated night counterpart who has worked the structure. Personnel are equipped with the personal protective equipment and business vehicles required, including Type 3 garments for night work, and no inspector works a night closure without having walked the closure plan in daylight first.

### 1.4 Communication with stakeholders

Communication on this contract runs in three directions and each has a defined channel. With the **Area Engineer**, a standing weekly coordination meeting plus same-day written notice of any deficiency or differing site condition. With the **contractor**, daily field coordination through ELIT leads with all direction on contract matters routed through the Area Engineer rather than issued in the field. With **adjacent projects and third parties** — utility owners, other consultants, the travelling public — a single point of contact in our Project Manager so that lane closure and haul route conflicts are resolved between projects rather than escalated.

Our public involvement lead maintains the contact list and comment record and supports the district on any public-facing communication, so that a corridor with this much visibility does not generate surprises.

## 3. Project Planning and Management
*Budget: 3.87 pages / ~2,130 words — the heaviest-weighted criterion*

### 3.1 Staffing: who, how, and why

**The structure.** Five inspection teams, four organised by direct connector and one covering roadway widening and overlay, each under an ELIT lead reporting to the Project Manager. Shared specialists — materials, geotechnical, survey, utility, environmental — are dispatched by activity across all five.

**Why this structure and not a pool.** A connector's foundation, substructure and superstructure are one continuous engineering story. The inspector who saw the shaft installed knows what the column is sitting on. Rotating staff through an undifferentiated pool discards that knowledge every shift and pushes the cost of rediscovering it onto the record. The specialist functions are the opposite: materials testing and survey are demand-driven and would idle if dedicated per structure. So the structure is dedicated where continuity has value and shared where utilisation does.

**Resource management against the contractor's schedule.** Staffing is driven by the contractor's three-week look-ahead, reviewed weekly. Each look-ahead is converted into a staffing requirement by activity, and gaps are resolved a full week before the work. Where the look-ahead shows concurrent placements on more than two connectors, we bring in additional inspection capacity from within the prime rather than reducing coverage.

**Peak and night coverage.** Deck placements and girder erection are the peak demands and both occur under closure. Each connector team carries a designated night counterpart. Night assignments are planned into the roster at the start of each month rather than filled reactively, because reactive night staffing is how inspection coverage quietly thins.

### 3.2 Retaining key personnel and continuity through turnover

The RFP asks directly about retention and continuity if key staff turn over. We answer it in three parts, because a commitment without a mechanism is not an answer.

**Retention.** Every proposed key staff member is a current employee of the prime or a named subprovider, and each has been informed of the specific role proposed for them on this contract. Our project assignments run for the duration of a structure rather than being reshuffled quarterly, which is itself a retention measure — inspectors leave rotating assignments more often than they leave stable ones.

**Depth behind each position.** Every ELIT lead position has a named second who has worked the same structure. This is the actual continuity mechanism: not a promise to replace someone quickly, but a person already present who does not need to learn the structure. Our Project Manager, bridge lead and superintendent lead each carry a designated second within the prime firm.

**Replacement discipline.** Under Section 17 of this solicitation the team structure must remain intact until contract execution, and a replacement for a key position must come from the same firm as the person replaced. Our depth plan is built to satisfy that constraint: each second sits inside the same firm as the person they back up, so a replacement request does not require restructuring the team.

**Knowledge that survives a departure.** The daily log, deficiency record and photographic record are maintained in a shared system rather than in individual notebooks, so a departure costs us a person and not a project history.

### 3.3 Quality control

Our Quality Control Plan will meet the requirements of Attachment C and will be submitted for approval before work begins. Its substantive elements:

- **A named person responsible for quality**, with current contact information, who is not the Project Manager and who has the standing to stop work.
- **Certification currency for every individual** performing specified testing and inspection, held electronically and reviewed monthly against expiry — an inspector whose certification lapses mid-project is a finding waiting to happen.
- **Proficiency sample participation** scheduled in advance of the placement windows that depend on it.
- **Equipment calibration** records, an annual calibration schedule, a maintenance and repair plan, and copies of the standards used — with calibration status confirmed before each testing campaign rather than at audit.
- **A defined procedure for accusations of misconduct** covering neglect, abuse or breach of testing integrity, and a defined procedure for protecting the integrity of quality assurance data.
- **Test result reporting within the time limits** set by the plan, with escalation when a result is late rather than silence.
- **Subprovider scope definition** — a maintained list of subprovider firms and their defined scope of responsibility, so that no activity sits between two firms unowned.

### 3.4 Use of subproviders

**Experience meeting commitments.** Ocotillo has led teams with six subproviders on comparable inspection contracts and has not replaced a subprovider mid-contract. We attribute that to how the team is assembled: subproviders are selected for the categories they hold and the capacity they can actually commit, not for the certification they carry on paper.

**How subproviders are utilised here.** Six firms hold twelve of the twenty-seven work categories at 36% of the work: materials testing and plant inspection, geotechnical, survey and control, utility adjustment and construction management, environmental inspection, and public involvement. The prime self-performs 64%, well above the 30% minimum, and retains all four of the categories carrying the largest shares of work.

**Managing them.** Each subprovider has one named task leader who attends the weekly coordination meeting for the weeks their work is active. Scope boundaries are written into the QCP rather than left to custom. Invoicing and progress are reconciled monthly against the same look-ahead used for staffing, so a subprovider falling behind is visible in the schedule before it is visible in the billing.

### 3.5 Project controls and administration

**Schedule.** Our scheduling project manager maintains an independent assessment of the contractor's critical path, updated with each schedule submission, so that the State's understanding of float does not depend solely on the contractor's representation of it.

**Change orders and claims.** Every proposed change is evaluated for cost and for time simultaneously. The State receives a single recommendation carrying both. Our claims lead is engaged from the start rather than called in when a dispute has already formed, which is the point at which the contemporaneous record either exists or does not.

**Systems access.** We will complete Forms 1828, 1980 and 2526 for all personnel requiring access to TxDOT systems, and we will initiate them at notice to proceed rather than at first need, because access delays are avoidable and they idle inspectors.

**Safety.** We provide personal protective equipment and business vehicles to all our personnel and require their use. Personnel comply with OSHA standards and TxDOT requirements including safety glasses and Type 3 garments for night work. Our training record for key staff — courses, completion dates, and the personnel list — is maintained and available.

**Closeout.** Documentation is assembled continuously and reconciled monthly. At project completion the closeout package is reviewed and submitted, not compiled.

### 3.6 Mobilisation

Mobilisation begins at notice to proceed and is complete before the contractor's first controlling activity. Three items are initiated on day one because each has an external dependency that cannot be compressed: systems access forms 1828, 1980 and 2526 for every person who will need TxDOT system access; the Quality Control Plan submission for approval; and confirmation of certification and calibration currency across the materials and testing function. Waiting for a need to appear before starting any of these is how inspectors end up on site unable to record what they inspected.

Our ELIT leads walk the project with the Area Engineer before construction begins, so that the first shift is not the first time our inspectors see the site.

### 3.8 Risk management

We maintain a written risk register from mobilisation, reviewed monthly with the Area Engineer. Four risks are on it from day one, each with a named owner and a defined trigger.

**Utility conflict during shaft or wall construction** — owner, utility task leader; trigger, any subsurface finding inconsistent with the plans; response, same-week work-around proposal.

**Differing site conditions in drilled shafts** — owner, geotechnical task leader; trigger, material variance from the geotechnical baseline; response, parallel technical and schedule-impact evaluation delivered as one recommendation.

**Concurrent-project closure conflict** — owner, Project Manager; trigger, any closure request overlapping an adjacent contract; response, resolution at the weekly inter-project call before either closure is confirmed.

**Materials testing capacity at peak placement** — owner, materials task leader; trigger, two or more concurrent placements in the three-week look-ahead; response, confirmed technician assignment and plant readiness before placement authorisation.

The register is not a formality. Each entry has a person, a condition that fires it, and an action that follows — which is the difference between a risk register and a list of worries.
